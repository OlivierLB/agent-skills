# Source-Guided Research

Find and use sources that fit the question. Check who produced each useful piece of information, what it establishes, and whether it applies to the user's context. Explain meaningful uncertainty and disagreement.

## Choose where to look

First identify what kind of answer the question needs: a current fact, an official rule, expert consensus, original evidence, historical context, practical experience, or a comparison of viewpoints. Then choose sources suited to that need.

Examples include:

- **Official rules, policies, and procedures:** laws, regulations, government or institutional guidance, and the responsible organization's own documents.
- **Health, science, and technical questions:** original studies, systematic reviews, professional or scientific bodies, and relevant official guidance. Match the evidence to the population, method, and question.
- **Current events:** reporting from accountable news organizations, direct statements or records, and independent reporting that confirms key details.
- **History and background:** primary documents where useful, alongside reputable scholarship that places them in context.
- **Products, services, and practical choices:** official specifications and terms, independent evaluation, and firsthand experiences that describe relevant circumstances.
- **Personal experience and community practice:** forums, reviews, interviews, and firsthand accounts. These can show what people encountered, but a single account does not establish how common or generally true it is.
- **A supplied document, project, or situation:** inspect the material itself and its relevant context before relying on general descriptions of it.

Do not search broadly by default. Use the most direct available sources first, then broaden the search when evidence is missing, the question requires multiple perspectives, or corroboration matters. For information that can change, check publication and update dates.

## Evaluate each useful source

1. **Origin:** Who created or published it? What expertise, access, or accountability do they have for this subject?
2. **Purpose:** Is it reporting, research, official guidance, advocacy, marketing, personal experience, or speculation? Consider how that purpose affects what it can establish.
3. **Evidence:** Does it show its basis, such as data, documents, methods, direct observation, or named sources? Separate evidence from interpretation and opinion.
4. **Relevance:** Does it address the same question, place, population, timeframe, definitions, and conditions as the user's situation?
5. **Currency:** Is it recent enough for this topic? Check whether rules, evidence, circumstances, or products have changed.
6. **Independence:** Are other sources confirming the finding independently, or repeating the same original source?
7. **Limits and counterevidence:** What qualifications, uncertainty, disagreement, or contrary evidence would change how much to rely on it?

Judge sources in context rather than applying a universal ranking. An official source is often best for what an organization currently says or requires; it may not settle whether a claim is empirically true. A firsthand account can establish what one person experienced, but not how common it is. A study can support a result within its methods and sample, but may not generalize to every setting. Explain these limits when they matter.

## Cross-check and synthesize

- For consequential, disputed, or surprising claims, seek more than one independent source when possible.
- Prefer sources closest to the original evidence. Trace repeated claims back to their origin where practical.
- Compare what sources actually cover before describing them as agreement or disagreement; differences may reflect dates, definitions, methods, or populations.
- Do not force consensus when credible sources differ. Describe the disagreement and what remains unresolved.
- Distinguish what sources state from your own synthesis or inference.
- If a source is inaccessible, indirect, or insufficient to verify a claim, say so and qualify the conclusion.

## Present the answer

Lead with the answer that best fits the evidence. Support important factual claims with links or precise references near the relevant text. Explain briefly why the sources are relevant and note material limits, disagreement, or uncertainty. Include dates and scope where they affect interpretation. Keep the source trail useful and concise; do not turn every answer into a bibliography.
